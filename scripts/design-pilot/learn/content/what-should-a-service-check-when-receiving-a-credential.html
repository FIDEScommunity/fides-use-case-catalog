Check the credential

Once you know what evidence your service needs, decide what it must check before relying on a credential. See Accepting information from a wallet.

In W3C terms, verification covers whether a credential is an authentic and current statement of the issuer: the securing mechanism is satisfied and, if a [fides_learn_term term="Credential Status"]status[/fides_learn_term] is present, the status check succeeds. The data model also lets an issuer express when a credential becomes valid and when it ceases to be valid. Status information may indicate that a credential has been suspended or revoked, where a status mechanism is provided and can be checked.

For qualified electronic attestations of attributes, the European Digital Identity Regulation provides that a revoked attestation loses its validity from the moment of revocation.

A service may also need to determine whether the person presenting the information is entitled to use it. How that check works depends on the technology and the situation. Whether a person may act for an organisation is a separate question; see How can someone act for an organisation?.

Decide whether the information is enough

Return to the supplier onboarding example. Your service receives a credential about the person submitting the application. It checks who issued the credential, whether it has been altered and whether it is still valid. Then your organisation needs to decide whether the evidence covers the action in question: can this person submit this application on behalf of this supplier? If the evidence does not establish that authority, decide what further evidence you would accept.

W3C makes the same distinction: verifying a credential does not establish that the claims in it are true. A verifier validates the claims against its own rules, and relies on them only after evaluating the issuer, the proof, the subject and the claims.

Make the request clear

Explain who is asking for the information and why. Depending on the wallet and credential, a person may be able to share selected information rather than every attribute in a credential.

For European Digital Identity Wallets, the Regulation requires a relying party to register in the Member State where it is established, to state its intended use of wallets including the data it will request, and to identify itself to the user. A relying party may not request data other than what it indicated at registration, and it is responsible for authenticating and validating the data it receives.
