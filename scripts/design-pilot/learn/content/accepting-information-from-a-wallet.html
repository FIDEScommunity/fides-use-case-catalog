Your organisation may ask people or other organisations for information before providing a service. A bank identifies a customer. An employer checks a qualification. A company asks a supplier for registration details.

A wallet can offer another way to receive that information. The holder presents a digital credential, or selected information from it, to your service. Your organisation then checks whether the information is suitable for the decision it needs to make.

This article explains what to look at once you have a service in mind.

Start with the service

Before choosing a wallet integration, look at the point in your process where information is needed.

For example, an employer wants to check whether an applicant holds a particular qualification. The employer does not necessarily need the applicant's full education history. It first needs to determine what must be demonstrated, which issuers it can rely on and what happens if the information cannot be provided.

[fides_learn_record type="usecase" id="gradus-verify-the-diploma-before-you-interview-obA7P8" url="{{pilot}}/use-case/" kicker="Demo with a fictional scenario" text="Gradus shows how a recruiter could check a digital diploma before inviting a candidate for an interview." label="Explore the use case"]

Discuss these questions with the people responsible for the service:

  • What decision are we trying to make?
  • Which information is necessary for that decision?
  • Who can provide or confirm it?
  • What should happen if the information is missing, expired or cannot be verified?

The answers will differ between services. They can show where wallet-based information could fit alongside existing ways of providing evidence.

What does a relying party do?

An organisation that requests and relies on information from a wallet is often called a [fides_learn_term term="Relying Party"]relying party[/fides_learn_term]. It decides what information its service asks for and how it uses the result. It also needs a way to check the information it receives.

The term [fides_learn_term term="Verifier"]verifier[/fides_learn_term] can refer to the party that receives and checks a credential, or to the software used to perform those checks. Depending on the solution, the relying party may use its own software or a service provided by another organisation. For the roles in one interaction, see How credentials connect issuers, wallets and services.

Verification and the business decision are related, but they are not the same step. A credential may be technically valid, while the organisation still needs to decide whether it is sufficient for its purpose.

What needs to be checked?

Start with the decision your service needs to make. Decide what evidence is sufficient for that decision. See What should a service check when receiving a credential? for the technical checks, and How can someone act for an organisation? if the request concerns someone acting on behalf of a company.

If you want a customer or employee to share information from a European Digital Identity Wallet, follow How can your organisation request data from an EUDI Wallet? When you need evidence about a supplier, decide separately what you need to know about the organisation and what shows that someone can act on its behalf.

Checking a supplier and its representative

When a new supplier signs up, you may need to check which company you are dealing with. If someone submits the application on the supplier’s behalf, you may also need to check whether that person has authority to do so. Evidence of the company’s identity answers the first question. For the second, check what the person is authorised to do on behalf of that company.

How does the person share the information?

A service presents a request to the wallet holder. The holder can review the request and decide whether to share the requested information.

The interaction may take place on one device or across two devices. For example, a person might use a wallet on their phone while accessing a service on a computer. The available interaction depends on the wallet, the service and the technical arrangements they support.

Decide when your service needs to check the evidence. Can you do it during the application, do you need to do it on site, or do you need a check at both points? Sharing information from a wallet explains how online requests and nearby readers work.

What about the EUDI Wallet?

Article 5f sets different acceptance rules for public services, certain private services and very large online platforms. The private-services rule applies where law or contract requires strong user authentication for online identification. Microenterprises and small enterprises are exempt from that rule. The platform rule applies where a very large online platform requires users to authenticate to access its online services. In both cases, the wallet is accepted at the user’s voluntary request. Check Article 5f for the conditions and timing that apply to your service.

For the rules on public services, see Accepting information from wallets in public services.

What about the European Business Wallet?

The European Commission has proposed a framework for European Business Wallets. It would support businesses in identifying themselves, sharing verified information and authorising people to act on their behalf, including across EU borders. The proposal is still going through the EU legislative process.

Prepare for a first discussion

You do not need to select a provider before looking at the process. Bring together the people responsible for the service, its information requirements, privacy, security and technology.

Map one interaction, from the moment a person is asked for information to the decision the service makes. Use it to discuss requirements with potential providers or partners.

See it in practice

Plan a verification check

A supplier based in another EU country applies to join your supplier portal. A member of staff submits the application on the supplier’s behalf.

  1. What is the minimum information you need to identify the supplier? Which source would you accept as evidence?
  2. What evidence would you accept that this person can submit the application for that company? How would you check that their authority covers this task?
  3. If you can verify the company’s identity but cannot confirm the person’s authority, how would you follow up?
