Describe one exchange

Pick one situation that occurs regularly and describe it as a single exchange: one set of information, one receiving service and one first test. For example, an employer asks an applicant to show a qualification. Keeping to one exchange makes it possible to see what a wallet would change and what would have to be agreed.

Which wallet your organisation uses, or whether it needs a business wallet at all, is a separate choice. For that, see Business wallets: where to start.

Who is involved?

Describe who has the information, who issues it, who holds it and who needs to check it. In the employer example, an education provider may issue the qualification, the applicant holds it and the employer checks it.

These roles can be carried out by different organisations. A provider may also supply software or support part of the interaction.

Which information is exchanged, and where does it come from?

Identify the decision the receiving service needs to make and the information it needs for that decision. Find out who is responsible for the source, how current it needs to be and what should happen if it is incorrect.

How does the receiving service check it?

Write down what the service will check and what it will accept as sufficient. See What should a service check when receiving a credential? for the checks behind that decision.

What happens if something goes wrong?

Decide what the service does when information is missing, expired or cannot be verified. Consider people who cannot or do not want to use a wallet, and how they can complete the process. For example, a recital of the European Digital Identity Regulation says that users should be under no obligation to use a European Digital Identity Wallet to access private services.

Also consider who provides support and how problems are resolved.

Agree the practical arrangements

Ask the parties involved how information can be issued and received, which formats and protocols are supported, and what agreements are needed. A shared format alone does not establish that two products will work together in your process.

Also consider how the service will be maintained once it is in use.

Plan the first test

Choose one first test with the people and organisations who will use the process. Write down the assumptions you want to test, such as whether the information can be issued, presented and checked as described. Then adjust the description before widening the scope.
