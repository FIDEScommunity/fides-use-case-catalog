The European Digital Identity Wallet, often called the EUDI Wallet, is part of a European framework for digital identity. It is intended to help people identify themselves and share information with public and private services across the EU.

The wallet can also hold other digital credentials, such as evidence of a qualification or a permission. Which credentials are available depends on the organisations that issue them and the services that accept them.

What can someone do with a wallet?

A person could use a wallet to identify themselves when accessing an online service. They could also receive a digital credential from an organisation and present information from it when needed.

For example, an education provider could issue a digital qualification. Its holder could then present it when applying for a job. The employer receives the information needed for the application and checks the credential. The exact process depends on the credential, the wallet and the service involved.

Who is involved?

Several parties have a role in a wallet interaction:

  • The [fides_learn_term term="Holder"]holder[/fides_learn_term] receives and shares information using a wallet.
  • The [fides_learn_term term="Issuer"]issuer[/fides_learn_term] provides the identity data or another credential.
  • The [fides_learn_term term="Relying Party"]relying party[/fides_learn_term] is the service or organisation that asks for and checks information.
  • The [fides_learn_term term="Wallet Provider"]wallet provider[/fides_learn_term] provides the wallet used by the holder.

An organisation can have more than one role. For example, a government body could issue a credential for one process and receive credentials in another.

Is there one European wallet?

Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 sets common requirements for EUDI Wallets. Member States are responsible for making at least one wallet available under that framework, and the European Commission has said this should happen by the end of 2026. Wallets may be provided by public bodies or by recognised private parties.

National implementation is still developing. Availability, onboarding and the credentials that can be used may differ between countries and change over time. National pages, such as the Dutch EDI-Stelsel or the German EUDI ecosystem, show how one country approaches this. They are not a uniform EU procedure.

What does this mean for organisations?

An organisation may want to accept information from wallets, issue credentials, provide wallet technology or support another party with an integration.

Choose one service or process to examine. What information does it need? Who can provide that information? What does the organisation need to check before acting on it? A technical check of a credential is not the same as deciding that the information is suitable for your service. That decision stays with the service.

Some organisations will also need to assess whether wallet acceptance requirements apply to their services. That depends on the organisation, the service and the applicable rules. There is no single acceptance duty for every organisation.
