The European Digital Identity Wallet, or EUDI Wallet, is part of the EU’s digital identity framework under the amended eIDAS Regulation. It gives people another way to identify themselves and share specific information with public and private services. The framework sets common requirements for wallets and for how participating services request information.

What can someone do with a wallet?

Someone renting a car could share details from a mobile driving licence if the rental company accepts it. A service checking an age limit could ask for an age result. The exact information someone can share depends on what has been issued to their wallet and what the service supports.

Who is involved?

Several parties have a role in a wallet interaction:

  • [fides_learn_term term="Holder"]The holder[/fides_learn_term] receives and shares information using a wallet.
  • [fides_learn_term term="Issuer"]The issuer[/fides_learn_term] provides the identity data or another credential.
  • [fides_learn_term term="Relying Party"]The relying party[/fides_learn_term] is the service or organisation that asks for and checks information.
  • [fides_learn_term term="Wallet Provider"]The wallet provider[/fides_learn_term] provides the wallet used by the holder.

An organisation can have more than one role. For example, a government body could issue a credential for one process and receive credentials in another.

A service that relies on an EUDI Wallet has to register and state what information it intends to request. When an intermediary acts on behalf of a relying party, eIDAS treats it as a relying party for these transactions. The intermediary must not store data about the content of the transaction.

Is there one European wallet?

Each Member State is responsible for making at least one EUDI Wallet available under the EU framework. Whether you can use a particular credential for a particular service depends on the issuers and services participating in that use case. The German programme and the Dutch EDI programme explain how this is being put into practice in their countries.

What does this mean for organisations?

An organisation may want to accept information from wallets, issue credentials, provide wallet technology or support another party with an integration.

Some organisations will also need to assess whether wallet acceptance requirements apply to their services. That depends on the organisation, the service and the applicable rules. There is no single acceptance duty for every organisation.
