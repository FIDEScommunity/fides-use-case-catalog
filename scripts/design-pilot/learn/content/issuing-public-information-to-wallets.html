Public bodies hold information that people often need to prove: where they live, whether they hold a licence or permit, or what a register says about a company. Issuing a credential from that information is one way to let people carry it to the services that ask for it. Public organisations in different countries ask similar questions before they start.

Questions to start with

  • Which information is the body responsible for, and is it an [fides_learn_term term="Authentic Source"]authentic source[/fides_learn_term] for that information?
  • Which credential should confirm it, and for which services?
  • Who may receive it, and how does the person get it into their wallet?
  • How is the information kept current, and what happens when it changes?
  • Where is the credential described, so that receiving services can understand it?

The register stays where it is

Information in a register or case system remains with the body that is responsible for it. A credential is a way to provide a statement from that source in a form a person can hold and share. It does not replace the register. The issuer remains responsible for what the credential confirms, including how errors are corrected and what happens when the underlying information changes.

The general questions for issuers are in Issuing credentials for wallets and The issuer. A credential type, which describes a kind of credential, can help receiving parties find out what information is defined.

Public bodies and the EUDI Wallet

In the European digital identity framework, attestations can be issued in more than one way. [fides_learn_term term="Public Sector Body EAA"]A public sector body attestation[/fides_learn_term] is issued by, or on behalf of, a public sector body that is responsible for an authentic source. [fides_learn_term term="Qualified Electronic Attestation of Attributes"]A qualified attestation[/fides_learn_term] is issued by a [fides_learn_term term="Qualified Trust Service Provider"]qualified trust service provider[/fides_learn_term]. Which route applies depends on the information, the national arrangements and the applicable rules. See European Digital Identity Wallet for the framework.

A Dutch example

The Dutch GBO draft design describes both routes for the wallet channel. In the first, a public body issues attestations itself and may use a shared facility for the technical side of issuing. In the second, a qualified trust service provider issues on the body’s behalf, after checking or retrieving attributes from the source through an agreed interface. According to the draft, the parties involved are still deciding which route is preferred, and the governance for that choice sits outside GBO.

The draft also says that GBO itself would not be the issuer of public attestations, that use of the shared facility is optional and that a public body may issue its own attestations outside GBO. Which organisation could provide the shared facility is described as still to be decided. Source holders may use reference components or an equivalent solution of their own, as long as they follow the agreements.

The draft marks the issuing facility for the EUDI Wallet as still to be realised. It describes a plan and not a service that is available today. Showing an attestation to a service takes place after issuing and is described as outside GBO.

The Dutch example is based on the Globaal Ontwerp (global design) of Gemeenschappelijke Bronontsluiting (GBO), version 0.9.5 (September 2026), a draft design submitted for adoption. Its arrangements are still in development and national arrangements can differ.

What can differ between countries?

Countries differ in which bodies may issue, which wallets are available, how issuers are onboarded and which agreements describe credentials. A listing in a FIDES catalog does not settle whether an organisation is allowed to issue. Check the rules and the responsible body in your own country.
