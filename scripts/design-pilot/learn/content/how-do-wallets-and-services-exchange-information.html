A wallet can receive information from an issuer and later share information with a service. The parties need ways to communicate, understand the information and check it. A protocol describes part of that communication. The difference between a format and a protocol is explained in Formats and protocols: what do they do?; this article follows one credential through the steps.

Start with an example

An education provider offers a digital qualification to a student. The student receives it in a wallet. Later, an employer asks for evidence of the qualification. The wallet presents information in response, and the employer’s service checks it before making its own decision.

The example is illustrative. The organisations may use different software, and the steps available to the user depend on the products and arrangements involved.

Receiving a credential

The issuer needs to make a credential available, and the wallet needs to request or receive it. The parties may need to establish which credential is being issued and whether the intended recipient is eligible to receive it.

OpenID for Verifiable Credential Issuance, often called [fides_learn_term term="OpenID4VCI"]OpenID4VCI[/fides_learn_term], is one specification for issuing credentials. It describes interactions between an issuer and a wallet, and the OpenID Foundation has published version 1.0 as a final specification (September 2025). An implementation must also support the credential format and the other requirements of the particular interaction.

Presenting information

A receiving service asks for information it needs. Depending on the wallet, credential and request, the holder may be able to share selected information. The service then checks the response and decides whether it is sufficient for its purpose.

OpenID for Verifiable Presentations, or [fides_learn_term term="OpenID4VP"]OpenID4VP[/fides_learn_term], is one specification for requesting and presenting credentials; version 1.0 is also published as a final specification (July 2025). Other protocols and interaction methods also exist. Which one is used depends on the ecosystem and the products involved.

What should organisations check?

  • How do parties identify each other and find the information needed to check a response?
  • What happens if a request fails, a credential is unavailable or status cannot be checked?
  • Which agreements and test environments apply to the use case?

Questions about the protocol versions and credential formats each product supports are covered in Formats and protocols: what do they do?. A product’s support for a specification is a starting point for a conversation. Testing the actual interaction with the parties involved remains important.
