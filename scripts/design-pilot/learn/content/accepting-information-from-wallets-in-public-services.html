Public services ask people for information all the time: identity, address, income, a qualification. Services in different countries rely on forms, uploads, registers or log-in providers. A wallet can add another way to receive information. The service still needs to decide what it needs, what it will accept and what it does with the answer.

Questions to start with

  • Which decision does the service make, and which information is necessary for it?
  • Is the information needed to identify a person, to confirm an attribute or to show a right or condition?
  • Which issuers does the service accept, and how does it learn about them?
  • What does the service do when a person has no wallet, or when information cannot be verified?
  • Which legal basis and data protection rules apply to what the service asks for?

Ask for what the service needs

A request can name specific attributes instead of asking for a full record, where the wallet and the credential support that. This is often called [fides_learn_term term="Selective Disclosure"]selective disclosure[/fides_learn_term]. It can help a service receive less information, which also leaves less to protect and explain. Whether a service may ask for particular information is a question for the applicable rules, not for the technology.

Checking is not deciding

A technical check can show who issued a credential, whether it was altered and whether it is still valid. It does not show that the information is sufficient for the decision the service has to make. That assessment stays with the service, together with what happens when a check fails. See The relying party and Accepting information from a wallet.

Does a public service have to accept wallets?

That depends on the country, the service and the applicable rules, and these are still developing. There is no single answer that fits every public organisation. The article The EUDI Wallet and national implementation explains where national arrangements come in.

A Dutch example

The Dutch GBO draft design describes three routes by which a service can obtain public data, each with its own parties, legal bases and protocols. In the wallet route, a person first receives an attestation from a public body or a qualified trust service provider. The person can then show it to a service. The draft describes that step as taking place after issuing and outside GBO.

The other two routes are different. In the OOTS route, a public service in another Member State requests evidence, and the person is described as reviewing and agreeing before it is sent. In the route for private service providers, the person gives consent for a specific purpose, recipient and data set, and the provider is described as receiving a party-specific pseudonym and not the citizen service number. A service should therefore not assume that a wallet presentation is the only way information reaches it.

The draft describes several components for these routes as still to be realised, and parts of the legal basis, such as the one for consent, as needing legislation. It describes a direction and not a finished service.

The Dutch example is based on the Globaal Ontwerp (global design) of Gemeenschappelijke Bronontsluiting (GBO), version 0.9.5 (September 2026), a draft design submitted for adoption. Its arrangements are still in development and national arrangements can differ.
