Public services ask people for information all the time: identity, address, income, a qualification. Services in different countries rely on forms, uploads, registers or log-in providers. A wallet can add another way to receive information. The service still needs to decide what it needs, what it will accept and what it does with the answer.

Questions to start with

  • Which decision does the service make, and which information is necessary for it?
  • Is the information needed to identify a person, to confirm an attribute or to show a right or condition?
  • Which issuers does the service accept, and how does it learn about them?
  • What does the service do when a person has no wallet, or when information cannot be verified?
  • Which legal basis and data protection rules apply to what the service asks for?

Ask for what the service needs

A request can name specific attributes instead of asking for a full record, where the wallet and the credential support that. This is often called [fides_learn_term term="Selective Disclosure"]selective disclosure[/fides_learn_term]. It can help a service receive less information, which also leaves less to protect and explain. Whether a service may ask for particular information is a question for the applicable rules, not for the technology.

Checking is not deciding

A technical check can show who issued a credential, whether it was altered and whether it is still valid. It does not show that the information is sufficient for the decision the service has to make. That assessment stays with the service, together with what happens when a check fails. See The relying party and Accepting information from a wallet.

What does Article 5f say about accepting wallets?

Article 5f of Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, which amends the eIDAS Regulation, is titled “Cross-border reliance on European Digital Identity Wallets”. For public services, paragraph 1 provides that where Member States require electronic identification and authentication to access an online service provided by a public sector body, they shall also accept European Digital Identity Wallets that are provided in accordance with the Regulation. Read in its own words, this means:

  • It applies where a Member State requires electronic identification and authentication to access an online service of a public sector body. A service that does not require this is not covered by paragraph 1.
  • It says “also accept”. The wallet is added to the means a service accepts. The paragraph does not say that a service may accept only wallets.
  • It concerns wallets provided in accordance with the Regulation. It does not mention other kinds of wallets.
  • Paragraph 1 does not name a date of its own. When wallets are available follows from other provisions of the Regulation and its implementing acts.
  • It concerns access and authentication. It does not say which attributes or credentials a service must ask for, how the service must be built or what it decides on the basis of the answer.

Other paragraphs cover private parties. Paragraph 2 concerns private relying parties, other than micro and small enterprises, that are required by law or by contract to use strong user authentication for online identification. They are to accept wallets no later than 36 months from the date the implementing acts referred to in Articles 5a(23) and 5c(6) enter into force, and only on the voluntary request of the user. Paragraph 3 concerns very large online platforms under the Digital Services Act that require user authentication. They are to accept and facilitate wallets, only on the user’s voluntary request and for the minimum data needed. The date for paragraph 2 therefore depends on when those implementing acts enter into force; check the current position.

This is a summary of the text, not legal advice. Read the Regulation and the national rules that apply to your service.

A Dutch example

The Dutch GBO draft design describes separate routes by which public data can reach a service: a wallet presentation after issuing, a cross-border evidence request through OOTS, and sharing with private providers on the basis of consent. A service should therefore not assume that a wallet presentation is the only way information reaches it. The design is still in development. More on the Dutch example, its version and status.
