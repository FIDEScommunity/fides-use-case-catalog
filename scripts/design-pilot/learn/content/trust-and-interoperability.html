A supplier sends a digital permit to a new customer. The customer may be able to check who issued it and whether it has changed. It still needs to decide whether that authority can issue the required permit and whether the permit covers the work in the contract. For the technical side, see Formats and protocols: what do they do?.

What does trust mean in this context?

A verification check can establish whether evidence is authentic and, where the relevant checks are supported, whether it is still current. The customer interprets the result and applies its own requirements. For example, a permit may be authentic and valid but cover a different activity from the one being contracted. The supplier also needs to know who is asking for the information and why. The roles are described in How credentials connect issuers, wallets and services.

What is interoperability?

Interoperability is the ability of parties and their systems to work together for an intended interaction. That can require agreement at several levels:

  • Technical: can the systems communicate and process the relevant formats and protocols?
  • Meaning: do the parties understand the information and its intended use in the same way?
  • Organisational: who issues, receives and maintains the information, and who is responsible for each step?
  • Legal and policy: which rules apply to the parties, the information and the service?

The necessary agreements depend on the process. Systems can exchange data successfully and still interpret it differently or apply different rules to it.

What are rulebooks and trust registries?

[fides_learn_term term="Attestation Rulebook"]A rulebook[/fides_learn_term] can describe how a credential type is intended to be issued, presented and checked, together with the rules and responsibilities applying to it. The content and authority of a rulebook depend on the ecosystem that uses it.

A trust registry can make information available about parties or services recognised in a particular context. What it contains, who maintains it and how it is used vary. A registry entry should be assessed against the purpose of the interaction; its presence alone is not a guarantee for every use.

Where does TS11 fit?

TS11 describes how catalogs in the EUDI Wallet ecosystem represent and exchange information about attributes and the rules for attestations. Some FIDES Credential Catalog entries show TS11-related metadata when it is available. Use those details to find and read the underlying specifications. The metadata records what is known about the type. Official listing and certification require separate checks.

How can organisations prepare?

Choose one interaction and identify the parties who issue, hold and receive the information. Agree what needs to be checked and which sources and rules the parties will use. Then test the complete interaction, including what happens when information is missing, a check fails or a service is unavailable.

A supplier may be able to present the same permit to several customers. Each customer checks whether it accepts the issuer and whether the permit is current and covers the work it needs. Agree in advance which status checks matter and what to do if the information is missing, out of date or temporarily unavailable.

The FIDES catalogs can help locate credential types, organisations and wallet listings for further investigation. The information shown depends on the sources and submissions available for each listing; it should be checked with the relevant parties before a decision is made.
