Suppose a supplier presents a digital credential to a customer. The customer's service may be able to read and check it, but the customer still needs to decide whether the issuer is appropriate and whether the information answers its business question.

This distinction matters when organisations plan an interaction or compare products. Technical compatibility is part of the work; so are the agreements about the information and the organisations involved. For the technical side, see Formats and protocols: what do they do?.

What does trust mean in this context?

Trust is not a single yes-or-no property of a wallet or credential. For a particular interaction, the receiving party may need to establish:

  • who issued the information and whether it has been altered;
  • whether the issuer is recognised or acceptable for the purpose;
  • what the information means and whether it is current;
  • what rules apply when the information is issued, presented or checked.

The holder may also need to understand who is requesting information and why. Different ecosystems use different rules and technical arrangements to support these checks. The roles are described in How credentials connect issuers, wallets and services.

What is interoperability?

Interoperability is the ability of parties and their systems to work together for an intended interaction. That can require agreement at several levels:

  • Technical: can the systems communicate and process the relevant formats and protocols?
  • Meaning: do the parties understand the information and its intended use in the same way?
  • Organisational: who issues, receives and maintains the information, and who is responsible for each step?
  • Legal and policy: which rules apply to the parties, the information and the service?

The necessary agreements depend on the process. Systems can exchange data successfully and still interpret it differently or apply different rules to it.

What are rulebooks and trust registries?

[fides_learn_term term="Attestation Rulebook"]A rulebook[/fides_learn_term] can describe how a credential type is intended to be issued, presented and checked, together with the rules and responsibilities applying to it. The content and authority of a rulebook depend on the ecosystem that uses it.

A trust registry can make information available about parties or services recognised in a particular context. What it contains, who maintains it and how it is used vary. A registry entry should be assessed against the purpose of the interaction; its presence alone is not a guarantee for every use.

Where does TS11 fit?

In the European Digital Identity Wallet work, EC TS11 describes interfaces and data formats for catalogs containing information about attributes and attestation rulebooks. The information can help people and systems find specifications and understand how an attestation type is described.

In the FIDES Credential Catalog, some listings show available TS11-related metadata. A listing can show which information is present and which is missing. The listing is not an official EU catalog entry merely because it contains that metadata, and its presence is not a certification of the credential, issuer or wallet.

Metadata is a starting point for checking the underlying sources. The status and scope of TS11, the source of each field and the available information may change; consult the relevant specification and source documents for the intended use.

How can organisations prepare?

Choose one interaction and identify the parties who issue, hold and receive the information. Agree what needs to be checked and which sources and rules the parties will use. Then test the complete interaction, including what happens when information is missing, a check fails or a service is unavailable.

The FIDES catalogs can help locate credential types, organisations and wallet listings for further investigation. The information shown depends on the sources and submissions available for each listing; it should be checked with the relevant parties before a decision is made.
