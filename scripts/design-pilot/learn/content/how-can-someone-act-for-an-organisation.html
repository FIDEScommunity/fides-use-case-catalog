Identity and authority are different questions

A credential might identify a person. Another might contain information about an organisation. Neither automatically answers whether the person can act for the organisation in a particular situation.

For example, a supplier's employee wants to open an account with a business service. The service may need to identify the employee and the supplier, then determine whether the employee is authorised to complete the registration. What evidence is sufficient depends on the process and the applicable rules.

European rules on electronic identification already allow for the identification of a natural person representing another natural person or a legal person. How that representation is established, and what it covers, is a separate matter that depends on the applicable law and on the service.

Identity, authority and mandate

Three words are easy to mix up. They describe different things, and sources do not always use them in the same way.

  • Identity is about who the person or the organisation is.
  • Authority is about what the person is entitled to do for the organisation in this situation. It can come from different places depending on the organisation and the applicable law, for example from the rules on a company's legal representatives, from the organisation's own rules or from a decision of the organisation.
  • A [fides_learn_term term="Mandate"]mandate[/fides_learn_term] is an authorisation given to someone to act. It is one way to describe an authority that has been granted, and it can be recorded as a document or as a credential.

The same word can have a narrower meaning in a specific framework. In the European Commission's proposal for European Business Wallets, for example, a mandate is the authorisation that the owner of a European Business Wallet gives to an authorised representative to act for the owner in operating the functions of that wallet. Other sources use "mandate" or "power of attorney" for a broader authority to represent an organisation. When you read a credential or a rulebook, check which meaning is intended.

A credential identifies its subject. W3C notes that verifiable credentials are intended as a means of reliably identifying subjects, and that authorisation is not an appropriate use of the data model without an accompanying authorisation framework. A mandate credential therefore needs rules around it: who may issue it, what it covers and how a service decides to rely on it.

Where can evidence of authority come from?

An organisation may record a person's role or authorisation in its own systems or use information from another source. A mandate can describe an authority granted to someone. Depending on the arrangement, the relevant information might be provided in a credential or checked through another service.

Authority can be limited by purpose, period or conditions. The organisation using the information needs to understand what the evidence covers and whether it is still applicable.

What do European sources say today?

European sources describe parts of this topic. They do not set a single rule for every organisation and situation.

  • The European Digital Identity Regulation lists "powers and mandates to represent natural or legal persons" among the attributes that Member States must make it possible for qualified providers of electronic attestations of attributes to verify against the relevant authentic source, where the attribute relies on an authentic source in the public sector. A recital also mentions such powers and mandates as an example of information that can be provided as an electronic attestation of attributes.
  • The EUDI Wallet Architecture and Reference Framework describes a dedicated representation attestation that could be issued to a natural person who represents another natural person. It also states that the topic of wallet units for legal persons was removed from the framework in view of the development of a separate business wallet.
  • Directive (EU) 2025/25 introduces a digital EU power of attorney for companies, based on a common template, to authorise a person to represent the company in specific procedures with a cross-border dimension. It is drawn up in accordance with national legal requirements and does not affect national rules on the use of powers of attorney. The directive also notes that company law already requires information on legal representatives to be disclosed in business registers, and leaves it to Member States whether a specific EU digital power of attorney must be filed.
  • The proposal for European Business Wallets would let an owner give authorised representatives mandates, and would aim to be compatible with the digital EU power of attorney. It is a proposal that is still in the legislative process.

Whether a particular service accepts a particular credential or document as evidence is for that service to decide under the rules that apply to it.

Questions to ask

  • Who is acting, and which organisation do they represent?
  • What action are they allowed to perform?
  • Who can confirm that authority?
  • How can a service check whether the information is current?
  • What happens if authority changes or ends?
