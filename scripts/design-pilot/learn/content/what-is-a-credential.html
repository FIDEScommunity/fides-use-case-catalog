You may be asked to show that you have a qualification, that an organisation holds a permit, or that you are authorised to act for a company. A credential is a way to convey information such as this from the party that provides it to someone who needs to check it.

A digital credential can be designed so that its recipient can present it and the receiving service can check its origin and integrity. In some cases, the recipient can use a wallet to hold and present it.

An example

An education provider confirms that a person has completed a course. It issues a digital credential to that person. Later, the person presents the credential when applying for a job.

The employer needs to understand what the credential says and check whether it accepts the issuer for that purpose. A successful technical check can help establish that the credential is authentic. The employer still needs to decide whether the qualification meets its requirements.

This is an illustrative example. The participants would need to agree how the credential is issued, presented and accepted. For the roles in more detail, see How credentials connect issuers, wallets and services.

Credential, verifiable credential and credential type

[fides_learn_term term="Credential"]Credential[/fides_learn_term] is a broad term for information used to establish something about a person, organisation or another subject. It does not, by itself, identify a specific technical format or legal status.

[fides_learn_term term="Verifiable Credential"]A verifiable credential[/fides_learn_term] is designed to support checks on information such as its issuer and whether it has been altered. Different standards and formats can be used for digitally verifiable information. The W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model is one of the standards used in this field. A credential should not be assumed to follow that model merely because it can be verified.

An individual credential contains information about a particular subject, such as one person's completed course. A [fides_learn_term term="Credential Type"]credential type[/fides_learn_term] describes a kind of credential. It can help others understand what information to expect and find relevant specifications or rules. The precise definition of a type depends on the ecosystem and specifications being used. In SD-JWT VC, for example, a type is identified by a string called [fides_learn_term term="vct"]vct[/fides_learn_term]. That identifier belongs to that format and is not a general definition of every credential type.

In the FIDES Credential Catalog, a listing describes a credential type. It is not an individual credential held by a person or an endorsement of every issuer using that type. A listing, or the metadata shown with it, does not confirm that a credential is qualified, legally recognised for a particular purpose or supported by every wallet.

What is an attribute?

An attribute is a piece of information about a subject. A name, a qualification, a permit or a power to represent an organisation can be expressed as an attribute.

The information requested should fit the purpose. A service that needs to establish whether someone meets an age requirement, for example, may need an age-related result rather than every detail in an identity document. What can be requested or disclosed depends on the credential, wallet, service and applicable rules.

What is an attestation of attributes?

Within the European digital identity framework, an electronic attestation of attributes, often shortened to [fides_learn_term term="Electronic Attestation of Attributes"]EAA[/fides_learn_term], is an electronic attestation that allows attributes to be authenticated. It is a term used in the eIDAS framework.

You may also encounter [fides_learn_term term="Qualified Electronic Attestation of Attributes"]QEAA[/fides_learn_term], a qualified electronic attestation of attributes. A QEAA must meet the requirements for qualified attestations and is provided by a qualified trust service provider. Its qualified status should be established from the relevant information and rules, not assumed.

The framework also addresses attestations issued by, or on behalf of, a public sector body responsible for an authentic source. These are a distinct category with their own requirements. An EAA, a QEAA and an attestation from such a public sector body should not automatically be treated as the same thing.

The applicable legal effects and obligations depend on the category and circumstances. Check the relevant legislation and authoritative information for the specific use.

What is PID?

Person identification data, or PID, is data used to identify a natural or legal person in the context of the European digital identity framework. It has a specific role and requirements within that framework.

PID and attestations of attributes can both be relevant in a wallet interaction, but their purposes and governing rules should be considered separately. Which data a service needs depends on the interaction and the applicable requirements.

Does every wallet credential have the same status?

No. A digital credential's technical format, issuer, purpose and legal status are separate questions.

A credential might demonstrate membership in a private association, for example, or convey information used in a regulated process. To decide whether it is suitable, a relying party needs to understand what it confirms, who issued it and which checks and rules apply.

Where do schemas and formats fit?

A schema can describe the structure of information and the fields a credential is expected to contain. Other specifications or rulebooks may explain the meaning of that information and the arrangements for issuing or using it.

[fides_learn_term term="Credential Format"]A format[/fides_learn_term] concerns how credential information is represented and processed. Examples encountered in wallet ecosystems include mDoc and SD-JWT VC. A format alone does not tell you whether the issuer is trusted for your purpose or whether a credential has qualified status.
