People regularly have to show a public authority, or another organisation, information that a public body holds. Examples are where someone lives, whether they hold a permit or licence, or a qualification awarded by a public institution. Services in different countries ask for this in different ways: with documents, forms, uploads or a separate log-in.

A digital wallet can offer another way for that information to travel. A public body issues it, the person keeps it in a wallet and a service asks for what it needs. This article lists questions that public organisations can ask before deciding where wallets fit. For the basics, see What is a digital wallet?

Which question are you asking?

The starting point is rarely the wallet. It is usually a service or a piece of information. Questions that come up in different countries include:

  • Which information do people regularly have to prove, and which public body is responsible for it?
  • Which services ask for the same information again and again?
  • Can the responsible body confirm that information in a form a wallet can hold?
  • Does the service need a full record, or only one fact from it?
  • What happens for people who have no wallet, or who prefer another route?

These questions can show where a wallet could add a route next to existing ones. They can also show where it would not.

Two sides of the same interaction

A public body can take more than one role. As an [fides_learn_term term="Issuer"]issuer[/fides_learn_term] it provides a credential, for example about a permit. As a [fides_learn_term term="Relying Party"]relying party[/fides_learn_term] it can receive credentials from people, for example when someone applies for a service. The two sides raise different questions, covered in Issuing public information to wallets and Accepting information from wallets in public services. The roles themselves are explained in Ecosystem roles.

The issuing authority confirms the information. The receiving service checks the evidence and decides what it means for the application.

Wallets are one route among several

Public information is also exchanged without a wallet. Public bodies can exchange data with each other through registers and agreed interfaces. For cross-border procedures, the European Once-Only Technical System (OOTS) is described as letting a person ask authorities in one Member State to send evidence to a public service in another. Wallets, registers and cross-border exchange can exist side by side, with different parties and rules for each.

What can differ between countries?

Several things are decided nationally or by sector: which bodies may issue which credentials, which wallets are available, how organisations are onboarded, which registers are used and when services accept wallets. Do not assume that arrangements described for one country apply in another.

A Dutch example

The Dutch GBO draft describes wallet-based exchange alongside other routes for sharing public data. See The EUDI Wallet and national implementation for the Dutch example and its current status.
